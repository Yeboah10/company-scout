How Company Scout works

What it does, what it will not do, and how to read it.

The question it answers

You come across a company — in an article, a pitch deck, a conversation — and you want to know whether it is worth your time. Not a summary of their website. An assessment.

Company Scout takes a name and comes back with an evidence-backed brief. Every claim in it traces to a dated source you can open and check. It is built for African companies, because that is where the general-purpose tools are weakest — they rank syndicated press releases above the outlets that actually cover these markets.

It is designed to be capable of saying no. A tool that finds every company interesting is not assessing anything.

What you can use it for

Deciding what to write about

Whether there is a real story here — a business model worth explaining, a trajectory worth following, a failure worth documenting.

Case study research

The claims, the numbers and the sources are laid out with dates attached, so the groundwork is done before you start writing.

Finding a way in

Published addresses, likely address formats and LinkedIn links — with guesses labelled as guesses, never mixed in with confirmed contacts.

Preparing for a conversation

Recent developments, leadership, funding history and what has gone wrong, in one place, before a call or a meeting.

How a scout actually runs

Six stages. Roughly 100–300 seconds, depending on how much has been written about the company.

  1. Resolve the company. Work out which company you actually mean. “MAX” collides with a dozen others, so this step commits to one and says which.
  2. Search deliberately. Eleven searches, nine of them tied to a specific question — including one that only ever asks what has gone wrong.
  3. Extract evidence. Pull individual claims out of the pages, each tied to the source that supports it. If nothing usable comes back, the run stops rather than writing a confident brief from nothing.
  4. Analyse. Strategic signals, story angles, case study potential, outreach potential.
  5. Score. Two numbers, explained below.
  6. Find a route in. Published addresses, address formats, LinkedIn links.

The two scores

One number could not hold both of these, so there are two. They are genuinely independent.

Worth your attention

Is there a story, a case study, or research value here? A company that collapsed can score very highly — documenting how something failed is often more useful than another success story.

Can you reach them

Is there a route to an actual person, and is the company still trading? This drops to zero for a company that has wound down, however interesting it is.

Why both matter. 54gene, the Nigerian genomics startup, scores 8.5 for attention and 1.0 for reach. Worth writing about; nobody left to contact. A single blended score of 4.75 would have told you nothing useful about either.

Coverage — what it admits it did not find

Every brief reports which of the nine research questions came back empty. If nothing was found about how a company makes money, the brief says so rather than leaving you to notice the absence.

This is deliberately a fact about the public record, not a verdict on the company. Plenty of good businesses have simply never had their economics written about.

Contacts: found, inferred, candidate

These are kept in separate lists and never merged, because a guess that looks like a confirmed address is how someone ends up sending a personalised pitch to a stranger.

  • Found — published somewhere public, with the page that published it, and the date it was seen.
  • Inferred — built from an address format actually observed at that company. Unverified.
  • Candidate — the formats companies generally use, applied to a known name. Weakest of the three, and labelled as such.

Where a LinkedIn profile is not found, the brief says so and gives you a search link, so you know whether to look manually or move on.

What it will not tell you

Worth knowing before you rely on it.

  • It only knows what has been published. A company that keeps a low profile will produce a thin brief, and that is a fact about coverage rather than about the company.
  • Roles carry dates for a reason. Somebody described as an executive was described that way by a source on a particular day. Check before you contact them.
  • Inferred addresses are guesses. They are labelled everywhere they appear. Verify before sending anything that matters.
  • It is not financial or investment advice. It is a research shortcut, not a recommendation.
  • There is a daily limit. This runs on free infrastructure, which allows roughly three fresh companies a day across everyone using the site. Reports already saved open instantly and cost nothing — the home page shows what is left.

Why it is slow the first time

A fresh scout runs eleven searches and several rounds of analysis, so it takes a few minutes. The server also sleeps when nobody is using it, which adds around thirty seconds to the first request after a quiet spell. A report that has been scouted before opens instantly.
